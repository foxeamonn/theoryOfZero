Introducing Point zero.

In any system of forces, there will be one point where the force acting will be zero. The point most people will be familiar with is the fulcrum of a lever. For an body or object, this will be it's centre of mass, and for two bodies not in contact with each other, this will be their barycentre.

The law of the lever.

"Give me a lever and a place to stand, and I shall move the world". Thus spake Archimedes. What he was talking about of course, was the relationship between force and distance. The greater the distance of the applied force from the fulcrum, the more magnified the input force becomes, and the less the output force is. Regardless of the applied force, the gradient (output force / input force) will follow an inverse square law. Applied force as potential energy, output force as kinetic energy.

In the previous examples, the lever connected the effort and load output forces. But what if there was no lever? In that case force could not be transferred, and so it will exist as in potential form only. Consider a man digging a hole. The longer the handle of the spade, the force he will need to apply will be less, but the distance the dirt moves will be less - as per the law of the lever. If he puts down the spade he won't be able to dig of course, so his energy becomes potential. When he takes the spade up again, he will have to apply the same force as before to move the dirt - the ratio of applied force to output force is the same regardless of whether there is a mechanism to transmit it or not. This fact can be used to calculate the distance between points. As the dirt moves, it has kinetic energy, so kinetic energy can be considered analagous to output force.

Inertial motion and conservation of energy.

As shown in the previous diagram, if there is no pyhsical connection between two bodies, then the forces become potential energy. When a force moves along a lever (and is kept the same), the ratio of applied force to output force changes - but the nett force at the fulcrum remains zero. The same principle applies to energy; as the distance from point zero changes, both a body's potential and kinetic energies change. The total energy a body has consists of both kinetic and potential energy. As the distance it is from point zero increases, its' potential energy increases, and kinetic decreases. At all times %p.e + %k.e = 1. The instantaeneous rate of change (Gradient) can be given by the formula k.e/p.e or 1/p.e2. Thus inertial motion can be described as the change in a body's energy profile.

Mass, distance and energy.

As stated earlier, the instantaeneous rate of change of energy (Δe) will be 1/p.e2. The p.e of a body is going to depend on its' mass; so the greater the mass of a body, the greater the p.e of a body at a given distance. The formula m1*d1=m2*d2 shows this; the greater the mass of a body, the less distance it will travel from the barycentre to keep the system balanced. So distance can be described as the ratio of p.e to k.e. As the nett energy of the system must remain constant (zero), then Δe / mass must also be a constant; let's call it G for traditions sake.

Frames of reference.

The standard way of creating a frame of reference is to consider an observer to be stationary relative to a body they are observing moving. This is impossible of course; any real observer will be moving relative to the body they are observing. Furthermore, it is impossible for only one body in a system to be in motion. This implies that all bodies in a system (and consequently all bodies in existance) have both kinetic and potential energy. As seen earlier, the percentages will depend on distance from the barycentre.
To an observer in a standard frame of reference, the origin of the co-ordinate system is their own centre of mass. But because two bodies are moving the origin of the co-ordinate system is the barycentre of the observer and the mass point the body being observed. Acceleration.

When a body accelerates, its energy levels are changing (which is why it can be detected). This change in a body's energy changes the position of its barycentre with other bodies, and thus the point which it is moving relative to. The example below focuses on the change in the position of the barycentre between body A and B when body B accelerates. When body B's energy levels increase, the barycentre moves closer to body B. This increases the distance travelled by body A. When body B's energy levels decrease, the barycentre moves closer to body A. Neither observer is aware of the change in the position of the barycentre, but observer B will feel the acceleration as his energy levels change, whereas observer A's do not. After Body B has finished accelerating, the barycentre will again be stationary, and inertial motion will resume relative to it. The energy level of body B has changed so the amount of work it does over a given distance (its mass) will also have changed.
