Introducing Point zero.

In any system of forces, there will be one point where the force acting will be zero. The point most people will be familiar with is the fulcrum of a lever. For an body or object, this will be it's centre of mass, and for two bodies in motion relative to each other is their barycentre.

The law of the lever.

A lever is in equilibrium when the moment of the force on one side equals the moment of the force on the other side, i.e. force x distance on one side of the fulcrum equals force x distance on the other. The simple table below shows this relationship.

Applied force as potential energy.

In the previous examples, the lever connected the effort and load output forces. But what if there was no lever? In that case force could not be transferred, and so it will exist as in potential form only. Consider a man digging a hole with a spade. He will need to apply a certain force to move the dirt. If he puts the spade down he won't be able to dig of course, so his energy becomes potential. When he takes the spade up again, he will have to apply the same force as before to move the dirt - the ratio of applied force to input force is the same regardless of whether there is a mechanism to transmit it or not.

Inertial motion and conservation of energy.

As shown in the previous diagram, if there is no pyhsical connection between two bodies, then the forces become potential energy. When a force moves along a lever (and is kept the same), the ratio of applied force to input force changes - but the nett force at the fulcrum remains zero. The same principle applies to energy; as the distance from point zero changes, both a body's potential and kinetic energies change. As the distance it is from point zero increases, its' potential energy increases, but for energy to be conserved, its' kinetic energy must decrease. Thus inertial motion can be described as the change in a body's energy profile.

Inertial motion, mass, distance and energy.

For a body in inertial motion, its' energy profile changes. The amount of change over a given distance will depend on the mass of the body. The general formula for energy change is Δenergy = (Universal constant) * mass / distance or in shorthand Δe = GM/d. So the mass of a body (m) an be given by the formula m = Δe / (G*d) and the distance (d) from the barycentre as d = (G * m) / Δe. Figure 6 shows two bodies moving away from their barycentre. As G is a constant, it can be factored out giving the familiar m1*d1=m2*d2 formula.

Frames of reference.

The standard way of creating a frame of reference is to consider an observer to be stationary relative to a body they are observing moving. This is impossible, of course; any real observer will be moving relative to the body they are observing. To an observer in their own frame of reference, the origin of the co-ordinate system is their own centre of mass. In actual reality, the origin of the co-ordinate system is the barycentre of the observer and the mass point of the body they are observing (any real observer has to have mass, and thus can never be at the barycentre of themselves and the body they are observing moving). Acceleration.

When a body accelerates, its energy levels are changing (which is why it can be detected). This change in a body's energy changes the position of its barycentre with other bodies, and thus the point which it is moving relative to. The example below focuses on the change in the position of the barycentre between body A and B when body B accelerates. When body B's energy levels increase, the barycentre moves closer to body B. This increases the distance travelled by body A. When body B's energy levels decrease, the barycentre moves closer to body A. Neither observer is aware of the change in the position of the barycentre, but observer B will feel the acceleration as his energy levels change, whereas observer A's do not. After Body B has finished accelerating, the barycentre will again be stationary, and inertial motion will resume relative to it. The energy level of body B has changed so the amount of work it does over a given distance (its mass) will also have changed.
