Introducing Point zero.

In any system of forces, there will be one point where the force acting will be zero. The point most people will be familiar with is the fulcrum of a lever. For an body or object, this will be it's centre of mass, and for two bodies not in contact with each other, this will be their barycentre.

The law of the lever.

"Give me a lever long enough and a place to stand, and I shall move the world". Thus spake Archimedes. What he was talking about of course, was the relationship between force and distance. The greater the distance of the applied force from the fulcrum, the more magnified the input force becomes, and the less the output force is. Regardless of the applied force, the gradient (output force / input force) will follow an inverse square law. Applied force as potential energy.

In the previous examples, the lever connected the effort and load output forces. But what if there was no lever? In that case force could not be transferred, and so it will exist as in potential form only. As energy is the ability to do work, the applied force can be considered input energy and the input force potential energy. The relationship follows the same rule as forces; input energy (i.e) * distance = potential energy (p.e).

Inertial motion and conservation of energy.

When a constant force moves along a lever the ratio of applied force to input force changes. If potential energy is to be considered analagous, then as a body moves relative to point zero its' potential energy changes which means that the input energy must change also - or else potential energy could be come infinite. Inertial motion can be described as the change in a bodies' potential energy, or the change in the ratio of input energy to potential energy.

Frames of reference.

The standard way of creating a frame of reference is to consider an observer to be stationary relative to a body they are observing moving. This is impossible of course; any real observer will be moving relative to the body they are observing. Furthermore, it is impossible for only one body in a system to be in motion. This implies that all bodies in a system (and consequently all bodies in existance) have both kinetic and potential energy. As seen earlier, the percentages will depend on distance from the barycentre.
To an observer in a standard frame of reference, the origin of the co-ordinate system is their own centre of mass. But because two bodies are moving the origin of the co-ordinate system is the barycentre of the observer and the mass point the body being observed. Acceleration.

When a body accelerates, its energy levels are changing (which is why it can be detected). This change in a body's energy changes the position of its barycentre with other bodies, and thus the point which it is moving relative to. The example below focuses on the change in the position of the barycentre between body A and B when body B accelerates. When body B's energy levels increase, the barycentre moves closer to body B. This increases the distance travelled by body A. When body B's energy levels decrease, the barycentre moves closer to body A. Neither observer is aware of the change in the position of the barycentre, but observer B will feel the acceleration as his energy levels change, whereas observer A's do not. After Body B has finished accelerating, the barycentre will again be stationary, and inertial motion will resume relative to it. The energy level of body B has changed so the amount of work it does over a given distance (its mass) will also have changed.

Mass, distance and energy.

When two bodies move relative to each other, the input force/potential energy change, but these energies continue to cancel each other out at point zero. Figure 7 shows an action/ reaction event; the distance each body is from their barycentre will depend on their mass. At all times, mass body one * distance body one - mass body two * distance body two = 0 (m1d1-m2d2 = 0). As the potential energies of the two bodies are the same, then the mass of a body is the change in its' potential energy over a given distance, (Δp.e)/d. This implies that the instantaeneous rate of change of change of energy must be a constant that is independent of the mass of the body. For the sake of tradition, I will call this constant G. So G = ∇p.e.

Mapping to a co-ordinate system.

Any co-ordinate needs an origin point, and the obvious point to use in a two-body system is point zero. The position of the mass point of each body in the system can be given by its' mass and distance from point zero. In a polar co-ordinate system, the position of a body is given by its distance and angle from the pole - point zero. The applied force always acts at a right angle to the input force; but the nett force remains the same. As the angle the force acts at remains the same, but the distance changes then the angle to point zero changes. When angle and distance change simultaenously, that is a spiral.
